\section{Simulations of SIGGMA Observations}
\label{sec:siggma_simulations}

In this section we describe the RRL measurements from the Survey of Ionized Gas in the Galaxy, Made with the Arecibo Telescope (SIGGMA) \cite{liuSIGGMASURVEYIONIZED2013} and estimate CHORD's ability to detect the H II regions reported by SIGGMA.\\

The SIGGMA survey covers a thin strip of the Galactic plane between Galactic longitudes \(32\deg \leq \ell \leq 70\deg\) and Galactic latitudes \(|b| \leq 1.5\deg\) with a frequency range of 1225 MHz to 1525 MHz. A plot of the SIGGMA data showing the overlap with CHORD is shown in Figure \ref{fig:siggma_with_chord_coverage}. The survey averages the intensity profile of 12 RRLs (H163\(\alpha\) to H174\(\alpha\)) and covers a velocity range of \(\pm 300 \km \s^{-1}\) with a \(5.1 \km \s^{-1}\) channel resolution and a 6-arcminute angular resolution. An inner-Galaxy data release \cite{liuSurveyIonizedGas2019} provides measurements of 244 known H II regions and an additional 79 candidate regions\footnote{Data available at \url{https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/cat/J/ApJS/240/14t}}, of which 14 and 12, respectively, are within the CHORD survey range. The H II regions visible to CHORD are plotted over the CHORD sensitivity map at \(900 \MHz\) shown in Figure \ref{fig:hii_regions_over_chord_sensitivity}, which shows that most H II regions are located in areas with high background noise. A subset of the visible H II regions is provided in table \ref{tab:siggma_candidates_subset} and a full table is available in appendix \ref{sec:siggma_appendix}.

The 12 stacked lines have an average frequency of 1375 MHz, so we define the peak line strength as the brightness temperature at 1375 MHz, \(T_{L}(1375\,\MHz)\). We can then obtain the peak line strength for each line in the CHORD frequency range by scaling from this reference frequency,
\begin{equation}
    T_{L}(\nu) = T_{L}(1375\,\MHz) \frac{1375 \MHz}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
We computed the single-day per-line SNR of each of the 26 H II regions by dividing peak line strength by the CHORD sensitivity. We applied a common frequency resolution of \(5 \kHz\) and used CHORD's native angular resolution for each line, based on the precise spatial location of the given H II region. A plot of per-line SNR for one observing day is shown in Figure \ref{fig:SNR_per_SIGGMA_line}. SNR is maximized between 500 and 600 MHz, which is expected for observations along the Galactic plane. The lowest SNR is around 0.5, while the highest is 24. The two very bright H II regions `G063.164+00.449' and `G061.473+00.094' are significant outliers, with SNRs of 24 and 10, respectively, and are omitted from Figure \ref{fig:SNR_per_SIGGMA_line}.

Next we analysed the impact of smoothing on SNR using a velocity resolution of \(5 \km \s^{-1}\) and a common angular resolution of 22 arcminutes as described in section \ref{sec:smoothed_snr}. The resultant SNR is shown in Figure \ref{fig:SNR_per_SIGGMA_line_smoothed}. We also include the locations of the 5, 6, 8, 12 and 22 arcminute angular resolution cutoffs as dashed vertical lines. The number of lines within each cutoff is 8, 14, 27, 42, and 79, respectively. When smoothing is applied, the high-frequency lines have the highest SNR, so we prioritize them for stacking.

We simulated the line-stacking process described in section \ref{sec:line_stacking} using both the native-resolution SNR of Figure \ref{fig:SNR_per_SIGGMA_line} and different smoothed resolutions. In the case of native resolution, we present results for the top 1, 15 and 60 lines of each H II region. The resultant SNR predictions for one observing day are shown in table \ref{tab:siggma_stacked_snr}. The lowest single-day SNR with 60 stacked lines is \(3.93 \pm 0.39\), which demonstrates that each region should be easily detectable by CHORD without sacrificing any precision through smoothing. Furthermore, each observing day is an independent sample, so SNR increases in proportion to the square root of the number of observing days. A true survey will observe each region between 9 and 36 days, meaning SNR is expected to increase by a factor of 3 to 6.

We also considered the case of prioritizing angular resolution over sensitivity by only including the lines which meet a given angular resolution cutoff. We selected angular resolutions of 5, 6, and 8 arcminutes, and smoothed the available lines to that cutoff. We also smoothed the velocity resolution to \(5 \km \s^{-1}\). The results are shown in table \ref{tab:siggma_stacked_snr_subset}. The results show that with a 6-arcminute angular resolution, the predicted stacked SNR per day is 3 or greater, which matches SIGGMA results if using 4 or more observing days. For an improved angular resolution of 5 arcminutes, at least 9 observing days are required to obtain an SNR of 6 or greater.
