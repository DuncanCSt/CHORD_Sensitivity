\section{Conclusions}

In this work we analyzed the RFI environment at the DRAO using a one hour segment of data from the DRAO RFI monitor. We found that each calibration segment necessitated an independent RFI label to account for changes in the gain factor. We applied spectral kurtosis to the integrated power and, after proper calibration, found that it closely matched theory. We validated the RFI labels against known RFI events and found strong agreement. We chose to consider a frequency bin clean so long as it was RFI free in at least 50\% of the time segments, and we chose to apply a Gaussian-weighted average over frequency bins to classify whether a given RRL would be visible. We found that a total of 79 RRLs are detectable and 37 are contaminated by RFI. We used the 79 detectable lines as the basis for all future detection analysis.\\

We derived the theoretical point source sensitivity and brightness temperature sensitivity of CHORD. We found that sensitivity increases at high declination since sources spend longer within the primary beam. We also found that sensitivity increases at low frequency, due to the larger primary beam. However, background emission is also higher at low frequencies, which reduces overall sensitivity. We concluded that the overall sensitivity is highly spatially dependent, and should be calculated per location and per frequency. \\

We showed how an inverse-variance-weighted average can be used to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio of multiple RRLs. We explored how stacked SNR varies by sky location due to the dominant effect of background emission along the Galactic plane. We discussed smoothing strategies which increase SNR at the cost of precision by averaging measurements to a lower frequency or spatial resolution. We showed that smoothing decisions change the expected SNR per line and thus change which lines are most important to measure. As with sensitivity, the optimal stacking strategy depends on sky location and on scientific goals, namely which of sensitivity, velocity resolution, or angular resolution is most valuable.\\

We modelled adding dishes to the main CHORD array to create the extension CHORD array. We identified 8 locations as potential sites and modelled all combinations of 1 or 2 locations, for 40 total combinations. We found that the synthesis telescope track in combination with the disturbed land south of CHIME represented the optimal locations for constructing extension CHORD. While these two locations do not represent the longest possible baselines, they work together to produce excellent UV coverage and a symmetric synthesized beam without any distortions. We found that adding 32 dishes to the recommended locations could improve angular resolution by 44\% while reducing sensitivity by 25\%, or could improve angular resolution by 63\% for a loss in sensitivity of 200\%.\\

Finally, we estimated CHORD's detection ability of H II regions by simulating the CHORD response on data published by the SIGGMA survey. We found that CHORD's weakest detections could match the corresponding SIGGMA SNR levels with a common 6 arcminute angular resolution after 4 observing days, and could improve angular resolution to 5 arcminutes with matching SNR levels after 9 days. We showed that CHORD can detect the bright H II regions measured by SIGGMA after just one day and in most cases using only one RRL. A limitation of our SIGGMA H II analysis is that we estimate the radio background using a global sky model, which could be underestimating the very bright but very local background emission from the H II regions. \\
