\chapter{Background}
\section{Interstellar Medium}
Stars are born in the coalescing gas and dust that permeate our Milky Way Galaxy, known as the interstellar medium (ISM) \cite{carrollIntroductionModernAstrophysics2017}. As stars evolve, they recycle material to the ISM through stellar winds and supernovae, enriching the ISM with new elements. To understand the lifecycle of stars, it is important to study the nature of the ISM. Beyond its importance in star formation, the ISM traces the structure and dynamics of our Milky Way Galaxy and other galaxies \cite{carrollIntroductionModernAstrophysics2017}, allowing for the study of galactic evolution. Finally, the ISM impacts astronomical observations of everything from nearby stars to distant galaxies, making its study a crucial component of many other areas of astrophysics. \\

Part of the ISM is interstellar dust, which absorbs or scatters visible and ultraviolet light in a process called interstellar extinction. Dust extinction acts like a veil, limiting the distance that visible light can travel, which limits the area we can study through visible light. Radio waves do not suffer from dust extinction, so radio observations can reveal regions of the ISM that are inaccessible to visible light. \\

The dominant component of the ISM is hydrogen gas, comprising approximately 70\% of the ISM by mass, with the remainder consisting of helium and trace amounts of heavier elements \cite{carrollIntroductionModernAstrophysics2017}. Most hydrogen in the diffuse interstellar medium is neutral hydrogen (H I), which contains one electron in the ground state. Neutral hydrogen is very stable and is generally incapable of producing emission or absorption lines by the transition of the electron between orbitals. The 21-cm line, which arises from electron spin reversals in neutral hydrogen, is the main method used to detect neutral hydrogen.\\

When neutral hydrogen absorbs a photon in excess of 13.6 eV, the electron is ejected from the hydrogen atom, producing ionized hydrogen (H II). When the electron recombines, it can cascade downwards through the energy levels, producing a number of low-energy photons. Some very hot stars, or regions of very hot stars, can produce sufficient radiation to fully ionize the surrounding hydrogen gas in what are called H II regions. For these regions to maintain local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE), the rate of ionization must equal the rate of recombination, which produces continuous emission of low-energy photons. We discuss emission in the radio range in more technical detail in section \ref{sec:rrl_emission}. \\

\section{CHORD Telescope}
The Canadian Hydrogen Observatory and Radio-transient Detector (CHORD) is a radio interferometer being constructed at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory (DRAO) in British Columbia, Canada. The telescope consists of a 512-dish core array of 6-meter dishes operating from \(300-1500 \MHz\). A 64-dish subset of the full array, called pathfinder, is expected to provide early science and testing data by late 2026. The full array is scheduled to be commissioned in 2028.\\

\hl{
CHORD is an interferometer, meaning it does not generate conventional images directly; instead, each pair of dishes measures a single component of the sky's 2-dimensional Fourier transform. Each Fourier component measures the intensity of a unique 2-dimensional sinusoid, with alternating sensitive and non-sensitive regions. Combining many Fourier components reconstructs a conventional image. CHORD consists of a highly redundant array, meaning multiple pairs of dishes measure the same Fourier components. This design reduces the total unique Fourier components, however, each component receives multiple independent samples, reducing measurement error and assisting with antenna calibration. As a result, CHORD is expected to achieve higher sensitivity compared to a non-redundant design.\\
}

As a drift-scan telescope, CHORD maintains a fixed pointing and measures continuously along the strip of right ascension (RA) that passes through its field of view as the Earth rotates. The full survey is then built up by repointing the dishes to different declinations. The full declination range accessible to CHORD is \( +20\deg < \delta < +80\deg\), which is planned to be fully covered over a 5-year survey \cite{vanderlindeCanadianHydrogenObservatory2019}. The full possible CHORD survey extent is plotted in Figure \ref{fig:chord_survey_extent}.\\

The instrument will have a native spectral resolution of 195.3 kHz, with the option of upchannelizing to higher resolution using a two-stage polyphase filter bank (PFB) \cite{bij2026chord}. We henceforth assume a spectral resolution of \( 5 \kHz\), corresponding to a velocity resolution of \(5 \kms\) at \(300 \MHz\) and \(1 \kms\) at \(1500 \MHz\). Beam characteristics of CHORD and pathfinder are discussed in detail in section \ref{sec:angular_resolution}.\\
