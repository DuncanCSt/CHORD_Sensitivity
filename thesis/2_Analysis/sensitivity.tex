\chapter{CHORD Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{ch:sensitivity_analysis}
We derive the instrumental point source sensitivity of CHORD in \autoref{sec:sensitivity}, the angular resolution in \autoref{sec:angular_resolution}, and the surface brightness sensitivity in \autoref{sec:surface_brightness_sensitivity}. We then incorporate radio background to obtain the overall sensitivity across the full CHORD field of view in \autoref{sec:background_noise}.

\section{Point source sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
The point source sensitivity of a radio interferometer is the minimum flux density of a point source that can be detected at a given signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The standard approach is to choose an SNR of 1, in which case the sensitivity is equal to the Root-Mean-Square (RMS) noise. We will use the terms sensitivity and noise interchangeably. The RMS noise can be theoretically computed using the radiometer equation \cite{condonEssentialRadioAstronomy2016}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}, ~\mathrm{single~pol}}
    = \frac{2kT_S}{A_e\sqrt{N(N-1)~\Delta \nu ~\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}}},
    \label{eq:radiometer_noise_rms}
\end{equation}
where \(k=1.38\times 10^3 \Jy \K^{-1} \m^2\) is the Boltzmann constant, \( T_s = 30\K\) is the expected system temperature of each antenna, and \( A_e = \pi(D/2)^2\cdot0.5=14.14 \m^2\) is the effective collecting area assuming a \(50\%\) illumination efficiency \cite{SPIEpaper}. The remaining quantities are the bandwidth \(\Delta \nu\) in Hz, the number of antennas \(N\) and the effective integration time \(\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}\) in seconds, defined below.

The physical antenna parameters of $T_s$ and $A_e$ are typically combined into a single quantity called the system equivalent flux density (SEFD), defined as
\begin{align}
    SEFD &= \frac{2kT_s}{A_e} \\
    &= 5868 \Jy.
\end{align}
The SEFD, in units of flux density, represents the flux density of a point source that would produce the same measured power as the system noise.\\

While equation \ref{eq:radiometer_noise_rms} provides RMS noise for a single polarization, CHORD will measure in dual polarization. The total intensity, denoted as a Stokes I measurement, combines both orthogonal polarizations. Since each polarization is independent, the noise is reduced by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) due to the averaging of two independent noise contributions. Thus, the one sigma sensitivity for Stokes I can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \frac{\mathrm{SEFD}}{\sqrt{2 \, \Delta \nu \, \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \, N(N-1)}}.
\label{noise_rms}
\end{equation}

% Defining the effective integration time
Equation \ref{eq:radiometer_noise_rms} assumes that the source is directly in the center of the primary beam, which is the power response pattern produced by the dish geometry. However, CHORD is a drift scan telescope, meaning that instead of tracking a source of interest, it maintains a fixed pointing and sources move through the beam as the sky rotates overhead. We define the effective integration time \(\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}\) as the equivalent time that a source would need to spend in the center of the primary beam to accumulate the same total power as it does given its drift path.\\

The CHORD primary beam is an Airy disk which has response pattern
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \left[\frac{2 J_1\!\left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\sin\theta\right)}{\frac{\pi D f}{c}\sin\theta}\right]^2,
\end{equation}
where \(J_1\) is the first order Bessel function of the first kind, \(D\) is the dish diameter, \(f\) is the observing frequency, and \(\theta\) is the angle from the beam center. The Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of the Airy disk is given by 
\begin{align}
    A(\theta_{\FWHM}) &= \frac{1}{2} \\
    \Rightarrow \theta_{\FWHM} &\approx 1.029 \frac{\lambda}{D}.
\end{align}
For simplicity we will model the primary beam as a Gaussian with a matched FWHM and ignore the sidelobes. The Gaussian beam response is given by
\begin{align}
    A(r)
    &= \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\
    &= \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{r}{\theta_{\FWHM}} \right )^2\right]
\end{align}
where $r$ is the angle from the beam center and \(\theta_{\FWHM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln 2} ~\sigma\) is the primary beam FWHM. A plot of an Airy disk overlaid with a Gaussian beam is shown in figure \ref{fig:airy_gaussian_comparison} and shows that the Gaussian is a good approximation to the Airy disk when considering only the main lobe.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/airy_vs_gaussian.png}
    \caption[Comparison of Airy disk and Gaussian beam]{Comparison of the Airy disk and FWHM-matched Gaussian beam for observing frequencies of 400 and 1200 MHz, demonstrating that a Gaussian is a good approximation to the Airy disk.}
    \label{fig:airy_gaussian_comparison}
\end{figure}

The radial distance \(r\) can be expressed in terms of the azimuth and zenith angles as
\[ r(t) = \sqrt{\theta(t)^2 + \phi^2}, \] 
where \(\theta(t)=\theta^*(t)-\theta_0\) and \( \phi=\phi^*-\phi_0\) are the angles in azimuth (EW) and zenith (NS) between the beam center \( (\theta_0, \phi_0) \) and a given sky location \(( \theta^*(t), \phi^*)\). We can further express the azimuth angle in terms of Earth's declination specific apparent angular velocity as \( \theta^*(t) = t\cdot\omega\cos\phi_0\), and assume for simplicity that \( \theta_0 = 0\deg \) (that the azimuth angle is zero at time \(t=0\)). Making the tangent plane assumption we will ignore curvature and assume that the local sky is flat, treating \( \theta, \phi \) as Cartesian coordinates.
\begin{equation}
    A(t) = \exp\left[-4\ln2~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\phi_0 + \phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right].
\end{equation}
The total power accumulated from a point source per day of drift scan is the integrated time which the source spends within the primary beam squared. The first factor of the primary beam comes from the natural antenna power response, while the second arises during map making during the conversion from Fourier domain to image domain. \\

We take \(t=0\) to be when the source is directly over the beam \(\theta(0) = 0 \) and compute \( \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \) as:
\begin{align}
    \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} 
    &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty A^2(t)dt \\
    &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty  \exp\left[-8\ln(2)~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\phi_0 + \phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right] dt \\
    &= \exp\left(-8\ln(2)\frac{\phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^\infty  \exp\left[-8\ln(2)~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\phi_0}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right] dt\\
    &= D(\phi)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8\ln(2)}}\frac{\theta_{\FWHM}}{\omega \cos\phi_0}.
    \label{tau_eff}
\end{align}
Here \(\theta_{\FWHM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln(2)} ~\sigma\) is the Gaussian beam FWHM, \(\omega = 7.29\times10^{-5} ~\rad/s\) is Earth's sidereal rotation angular velocity, and \(\phi_0\) is the source declination in degrees. The factor \(D(\phi) = \exp\left(-8\ln(2)\frac{\phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2}\right)\) is a term in the range \(\left]0,1\right]\) accounting for the attenuation of sources outside the beam path.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_primary_beam_attenuation.png}
    \caption[CHORD primary beam attenuation]{CHORD primary beam attenuation factor \(D(\phi)\) vs zenith angle \(\phi\) for different frequencies, showing that lower frequencies have wider beams and thus less attenuation for sources outside the beam path.}
    \label{fig:primary_beam_attenuation}
\end{figure}

Combining equations \ref{noise_rms} and \ref{tau_eff} yields:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\RMS} = \frac{SEFD}{\sqrt{2 ~\Delta\nu~N(N-1) }}~\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln(2)}~\omega~\cos\phi_0}{\sqrt{\pi}~\theta_{\FWHM}~D(\phi)}}
\end{equation}
This can be simplified and expressed in radians as:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\RMS}~[Jy/Beam] = \frac{SEFD}{143}~\sqrt{\frac{~\cos\phi_0}{\Delta\nu[Hz]~N(N-1)~\theta_{\FWHM}[\text{rad}] ~D(\phi)}}
\label{eq:combined_point_source_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_point_source_sensitivity.png}
    \caption[CHORD point source sensitivity]{CHORD point source sensitivity versus observing frequency for one observing day with 5 kHz bandwidth. Sensitivity is improved at lower frequencies due to the wider beam and longer effective integration time.}
    \label{fig:chord_point_source_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\section{Angular Resolution}
\label{sec:angular_resolution}
The angular resolution is the smallest angular scale that can be resolved by the telescope. Angular resolution is derived from the synthesized beam, which is the combined sensitivity profile after correlating all antennas. The standard angular resolution metric is the synthesized beam FWHM, which is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:angular_resolution}
    \theta_{FWHM} = \frac{\lambda}{D_{bl, EW}}, \quad \phi_{FWHM} = \frac{\lambda}{D_{bl, NS}},
\end{equation}
where \(D_{bl, EW}\) and \(D_{bl, NS}\) are the distances of the longest baselines in the east-west and north-south directions, respectively and \(\lambda\) is the observing wavelength which can be converted to frequency by \(\nu = c/\lambda\). We plot the angular resolution versus frequency for both CHORD and Pathfinder in figure \ref{fig:angular_resolution} showing that angular resolution improves at higher frequencies due to the shorter wavelength.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_pathfinder_angular_resolution.png}
    \caption{CHORD and Pathfinder angular resolution as a function of observing frequency.}
    \label{fig:angular_resolution}
\end{figure}

We approximate the synthesized beam as a 2D Gaussian with FWHM equal to the angular resolution in azimuth (\(\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\)) and zenith (\(\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\)) directions. The synthesized beam's Gaussian profile is given by
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta, \phi) = \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right] \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left( \frac{\phi}{\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}
The synthesized beam solid angle \(\Omega_s\) is the area subtended by the beam. The solid angle is obtained by integrating the beam response over all angles. For a Gaussian beam, the synthesized beam solid angle is given by
\begin{align}
    \Omega_s
    &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(\theta, \phi)~ d\theta d\phi \\
    &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right]~ d\theta \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\phi}{\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right]d\phi \\
    &= \frac{\pi ~\theta_{\FWHM}~\phi_{\FWHM}}{4\ln2}.
\end{align}

\section{Brightness sensitivity}
\label{sec:surface_brightness_sensitivity}
Surface brightness sensitivity, which is a measure of the telescope's ability to detect extended sources, is obtained by dividing the point source sensitivity by the synthesized beam solid angle
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\Omega [\text{Jy/sr}] = \frac{\sigma_{\RMS}}{\Omega_s}.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_pathfinder_surface_brightness_sensitivity.png}
    \caption[CHORD and Pathfinder surface brightness sensitivity]{CHORD and Pathfinder surface brightness sensitivity (\(\sigma_\Omega\)) using a 5 kHz bandwidth and one observation day. Sensitivity is worse at higher frequencies due to the smaller beam size, which reduces the effective integration time and synthesized beam solid angle.}
    \label{fig:surface_brightness_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

It is then converted to a brightness temperature by applying the Raleigh-Jeans limit
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:brightness_temperature_sensitivity}
\sigma_T [\text{K}] = \frac{\sigma_\Omega c^2}{2k\nu^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_pathfinder_surface_temperature_5kHz.png}
    \caption[CHORD and Pathfinder brightness temperature sensitivity]{CHORD and Pathfinder brightness temperature sensitivity (\(\sigma_T\)) using a 5 kHz bandwidth and one observation day. Sensitivity is still worse at higher frequencies but the trend is less pronounced compared to surface brightness sensitivity.}
    \label{fig:surface_temperature_5kHz}
\end{figure}
Figures \ref{fig:angular_resolution} and \ref{fig:surface_temperature_5kHz} show the inverse relationship between angular resolution and surface temperature sensitivity across the 300-1500 MHz band, representing the trade-off between beam size and integration time.  Sensitivity at higher frequency can be improved at the cost of angular resolution by convolving the final image to a larger beam, however low frequency angular resolution cannot be improved without increasing the maximum baseline length. Sensitivity is also improved at higher declinations due to the longer effective integration time, as sources move slower across the sky and spend more time within the primary beam. The sensitivity maps for a single observing day are shown in figure \ref{fig:system_only_noise}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \textbf{Brightness temperature sensitivity maps assuming empty sky}
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/system_only_noise.png}
    \caption[CHORD Brightness Temperature Sensitivity maps assuming empty sky]{Brightness temperature sensitivity (\(\sigma_T\)) in the CHORD range for different frequencies assuming empty sky. Sensitivity improves at high declination due to the longer effective integration time, and at lower frequencies due to the wider primary beam.}
    \label{fig:system_only_noise}
\end{figure}

\section{Sensitivity with Radio background}
\label{sec:background_noise}

Radio background contributes unwanted noise which degrades sensitivity. Radio background is received by the antenna and adds linearly to the total antenna temperature. Therefore, noise from background is calculated in the same method as equation \eqref{eq:combined_point_source_sensitivity}. In other words, SEFD for combined background and internal noise is taken to be the sum of antenna temperature and the background temperature,
\begin{equation}
    SEFD = \frac{2k(T_{ant}+T_{sky})}{A_e}.
\end{equation}

The radio background is obtained from the Global Sky Model \citep{zhengImprovedModelDiffuse2017}. This model incorporates all dominant sources of radio emission into a single all-sky map of brightness temperature from 10 MHz to 5THz. The model is accesable through the pygdsm package \citep{pricePyGDSMPythonInterface2016}. Plots are created using mollweide projections in Galactic coordinates using the healpy package \citep{zoncaHealpyEqualArea2019}. The full sky background maps are shown in figure \ref{fig:Radio_background_maps}.
