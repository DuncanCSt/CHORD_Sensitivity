\section{CHORD Sensitivity}

\subsection{Point source sensitivity}

The senstivity of a radio interfermoter is the minimum flux density of a point source that can be detected at a given signal to noise ratio (SNR). Choosing an SNR of 1, the sensitivity is equal to the RMS noise, which can be theoretically computed using the radiometer equation given by \citet[eq. 3.203]{condon_essential_2016} as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}, ~\mathrm{single~pol}}
    = \frac{2kT_S}{A_e\sqrt{N(N-1)~\Delta \nu ~\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}}},
    \label{eq:radiometer_noise_rms}
\end{equation}
where \(k=1.38\times 10^3 \Jy \K^{-1} \m^2\) is the Boltzmann constant, \( T_s = 30\K\) is the expected system temperature of each antenna, and \( A_e = \pi(D/2)^2\cdot0.5=14.14 \m^2\) is the effective collecting area assuming a \(50\%\) illumination efficiency. The remaining quantities are the bandwidth \(\Delta \nu\) in Hz, the number of antennas \(N\)---noting that \(N(N-1)\) is twice the number of baselines, since each baseline contributes two visibilities of uncorrelated noise---and the effective integration time \(\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}\) in seconds, defined below. Antenna parameters are obtained from \citet{chord_pathfinder_basics}.

The radiometer equation can be simplified by introducing the system equivalent flux density (SEFD) as

\begin{align}
    SEFD &= \frac{2kT_s}{A_e} \\
    &= 5868 \Jy.
\end{align}

While equation \ref{eq:radiometer_noise_rms} provides RMS noise for a single polarization, CHORD will measure in dual polarization. The total intensity, denoted as a Stokes I measurement, combines both orthogonal polarizations. Since each polarization is independent, the noise is reduced by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) due to the averaging of two independent noise contributions. Thus, the one sigma sensitivity for Stokes I can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \frac{\mathrm{SEFD}}{\sqrt{2 \, \Delta \nu \, \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \, N(N-1)}}.
\label{noise_rms}
\end{equation}

% Defining the effective integration time
The effective integration time \(\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the total time that a point source spends within the center of the primary beam, which is the area which a single CHORD telescope is sensitive to. CHORD is a drift scan telescope; it maintains a fixed pointing angle and sources move through the beam as the sky rotates overhead. The effective integration time is computed based on the length of time which a source will spend within the primary beam over a single observing day.
The CHORD primary beam is an Airy disk which has response pattern
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \left[\frac{2 J_1\!\left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\sin\theta\right)}{\frac{\pi D f}{c}\sin\theta}\right]^2,
\end{equation}
where \(J_1\) is the first order Bessel function of the first kind, \(D\) is the dish diameter, \(f\) is the observing frequency, and \(\theta\) is the angle from the beam center. The Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of the Airy disk is given by 
\begin{align}
    A(\theta) &= \frac{1}{2} \\
    \Rightarrow \theta_{\FWHM} &\approx 1.029 \frac{\lambda}{D}.
\end{align}
For simplicity we will model the primary beam as a Gaussian with a matched FWHM and ignore the sidelobes. The Gaussian beam response is given by
\begin{align}
    A(r)
    &= \exp\left(\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\
    &= \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{r}{\theta_{\FWHM}} \right )^2\right]
\end{align}
where $r$ is the angle from the beam center and \(\theta_{\FWHM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln 2} ~\sigma\) is the primary beam FWHM.
A plot of the Airy and Gaussian beam is shown in figure \ref{fig:airy_gaussian_comparison} and shows that the Gaussian is a good approximation to the Airy disk within the main lobe.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/airy_vs_gaussian.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the Airy disk and FWHM-matched Gaussian beam patterns for a 6 m dish at 400 and 1200 MHz demonstrating that a Gaussian is a good approximation to the Airy disk within the main lobe.}
    \label{fig:airy_gaussian_comparison}
\end{figure}

The radial distance \(r\) can be expressed in terms of the azimuth and zenith angles as
\[ r(t) = \sqrt{\theta(t)^2 + \phi^2}, \] 
where \(\theta(t)=\theta^*(t)-\theta_0\) and \( \phi=\phi^*-\phi_0\) are the angles in azimuth (EW) and zenith (NS) between the beam center \( (\theta_0, \phi_0) \) and a given sky location \(( \theta^*(t), \phi^*)\). We can further express the azimuth angle in terms earth's declination specific apparent angular velocity as \( \theta^*(t) = t\cdot\omega\cos\phi_0\), and assume for simplicity that \( \theta_0 = 0\deg \) and that CHORD is pointing at the zenith, thus \(\phi_0=\delta\) the CHORD latitude. Making the tangent plane assumption we will ignore curvature and assume that the local sky is flat, treating \( \theta, \phi \) as Cartesian coordinates.
\begin{equation}
    A(t) = \exp\left[-4\ln2~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\delta + \phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right].
\end{equation}
The total power accumulated from a point source per day of drift scan is the integrated time which the source spends within the primary beam squared. The first factor of the primary beam comes from the natural antena power response, while the second arises during map making during the conversation from fourier domain to image domain. \\

Assuming we take \(t=0\) to be when the source is directly over the beam \(\theta(0) = 0 \) then tau effective is computed as:
\begin{align}
    \tau_{eff} 
    &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty A^2(t)dt \\
    &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty  \exp\left[-8\ln2~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\delta + \phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right] dt \\
    &= \exp\left(-8\ln2\frac{\phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^\infty  \exp\left[-8\ln2~\frac{t^2\omega^2 \cos^2\delta}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2 }\right] dt\\
    &= D(\phi)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8\ln2}}\frac{\theta_{\FWHM}}{\omega \cos\delta}.
    \label{tau_eff}
\end{align}

where \(\theta_{\FWHM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln 2} ~\sigma\) is the Gaussian beam FWHM, \(\omega = 7.29\times10^{-5} ~\rad/s\) is Earth's sidereal rotation angular velocity, and \(\delta\) is the declination in degrees. The final factor \(D(\phi) = \exp\left(-8\ln2\frac{\phi^2}{\theta_{\FWHM}^2}\right)\) is a term in the range \(\left]0,1\right]\) accounting for the attenuation of sources outside the beam path.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{thesis/plots/sensitivity/chord_primary_beam_attenuation.png}
    \caption{CHORD primary beam attenuation factor \(D(\phi)\) vs zenith angle \(\phi\) for different frequencies, showing that lower frequencies have wider beams and thus less attenuation for sources outside the beam path.}
    \label{fig:primary_beam_attenuation}
\end{figure}


Combining equations \ref{noise_rms} and \ref{tau_eff} yields:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{RMS} = \frac{SEFD}{\sqrt{2 ~\Delta\nu~N(N-1) }}~\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln2}~\omega~cos\delta}{\sqrt{\pi}~\theta_{\FWHM}~D(\phi)}}
\end{equation}
Which can be simplified and expressed in radians as:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{RMS}~[Jy] = \frac{SEFD}{143}~\sqrt{\frac{~\cos\delta}{\Delta\nu[Hz]~N(N-1)~\theta_{FWHM}[\text{rad}] ~D(\phi)}}
\label{eq:combined_point_source_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Brightness temperature sensitivity}
A telescope's synthesised beam is the combined sensitivity across all antennas. The angular resolution is defined as the smallest angular scale that can be resolved by the synthesized beam, and is measured by the synthesized beam FWHM, given by
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{FWHM} = \frac{\lambda}{D_{bl, EW}}, \quad \phi_{FWHM} = \frac{\lambda}{D_{bl, NS}},
\end{equation}
where \(D_{bl, EW}\) and \(D_{bl, NS}\) are the distances of the longest baselines in the east-west and north-south directions, respectively.\\

The synthesized beam can be approximated as a 2D Gaussian with FWHM equal to the angular resolution in azimuth (\(\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\)) and zenith (\(\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\)) directions. The synthesized beam profile is given by
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta, \phi) = \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right] \exp\left[-4\ln2~\left( \frac{\phi}{\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}

The synthesized beam solid angle \(\Omega_s\) is the area subtended by the beam. The solid angle is obtained by integrating the beam response over all angles. For a Gaussian beam, the synthesized beam solid angle is given by
\begin{align}
    \Omega_s
    &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(\theta, \phi)~ d\theta d\phi \\
    &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right]~ d\theta \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-4\ln2~\left (\frac{\phi}{\phi_{\mathrm{FWHM}}} \right )^2 \right]d\phi \\
    &= \frac{\pi ~\theta_{\FWHM}~\phi_{\FWHM}}{4\ln2}.
\end{align}

Surface brightness sensitivity, which is a measure of the telescope's ability to detect extended sources, is obtained by dividing the point source sensitivity by the synthesized beam solid angle
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\Omega [\text{Jy/sr}] = \frac{\sigma_{RMS}}{\Omega_s}.
\end{equation}

It is then converted to a brightness temperature by applying the Raleigh-Jeans limit
\begin{equation}
\sigma_T [\text{K}] = \frac{\sigma_\Omega c^2}{2k\nu^2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Incorporating Radio background noise}

Radio background contributes unwanted noise which degrades sensitivity. Radio background is received by the antenna and adds linearly to the total system temperature. Therefore, noise from background is calculated in the same method as equation \eqref{eq:combined_point_source_sensitivity}. In other words, SEFD for combined background and system noise is taken to be the sum of system temperature and the background temperature.

\begin{equation}
    SEFD = \frac{2k(T_{sys}+T_{sky})}{A_e}
\end{equation}

The radio background is obtained from the Global Sky Model \citep{zheng_improved_2017}. This model incorporates all dominant sources of radio emission into a single all-sky map of brightness temperature from 10 MHz to 5THz. The model is accesable through the pygdsm package \citep{pygdsm}, which allows for easy querying of the background temperature at any given frequency and sky location. Plots are created using mollweide projections in Galactic coordinates using the healpy package \citep{healpy}. The full sky background maps are shown in figure \ref{fig:Radio_background_maps}.
