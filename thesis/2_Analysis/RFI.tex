\section{RFI Impact}
\label{sec:rfi_impact}

\subsection{Definition of RFI}
RFI consists of radio waves emitted from any Earthly source that can overcome or be confused with the target signal. Examples of RFI can include satellites, electronic equipment, and local broadband or narrowband communications. Unlike statistical noise, RFI cannot be removed through smoothing or time averaging because RFI is a distinct signal and not a random variable. Instead, frequency bins or time intervals corrupted by RFI must be identified and removed. \\

\subsection{RFI Impact on RRL Science}
RRL science is particularly sensitive to RFI for multiple reasons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RRLs are weak signals operating at or below the instrument's sensitivity level, so even low-level RFI is impactful.
    \item RRLs occur only at specific frequencies, so there is no option to shift observations to avoid RFI.
    \item RRL detection requires sensitive measurements across a wide frequency profile, so any RFI within the profile could corrupt the measurement.
\end{itemize}
With these considerations in mind, it is vital to understand the RFI environment surrounding each target frequency to inform RRL survey performance.

\section{RFI Detection Methods}
\label{sec:rfi_detection_methods}
\subsection{Excess Kurtosis for Non-Gaussianity}

Kurtosis-based RFI detection originates from the principle that true astronomical signals, along with thermal noise, should appear Gaussian, while RFI should not. Intermittent RFI, produced from lightning strikes, engine ignition, and radar pulses, is leptokurtic, having more outliers than expected in Gaussian distributions. Continuous RFI, produced from communication signals with defined power envelopes, is platykurtic, having fewer outliers. Kurtosis is highly sensitive to the outliers of a distribution, which tests Gaussianity specifically in the area where we expect to find RFI. Alternative statistical tests, such as the Shapiro-Wilk test, measure Gaussianity across the full distribution. In an empirical study of eighteen normality tests, \citet{joPerformancesSeveralUnivariate2016} found that the kurtosis test had the greatest statistical power\footnote{Here, statistical power denotes the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis of Gaussianity when the data are in fact non-Gaussian.} for symmetric data and the Shapiro–Wilk for asymmetric data; both ranked highly among the remaining sixteen tests overall. We expect leptokurtic RFI to be asymmetric, having a long tail of outliers, while platykurtic RFI should be symmetric. While we expect Shaprio-Wilk to have good statistical power on leptokurtic RFI, it is computationally expensive or impossible to implement without completely redesigning a radio receiver's streaming data pipeline. The Shapiro-Wilk test requires storing and sorting all accumulated samples per frequency channel; in comparison, the kurtosis test can be computed in a single streaming pass, which explains why kurtosis-based methods are preferred in astronomy \cite{mirhosseiniHighCadenceKurtosis2020}. While either test could be performed in this analysis, we use kurtosis rather than Shapiro–Wilk to simulate the compute constraints expected of the CHORD streaming pipeline.\\

Kurtosis is the standardized fourth central moment of a distribution and is a measure of how strong the tails of a distribution are. The $k$-th central moment of a distribution is defined by the expectation value $\mu_k \equiv \E[(X-\mu)^k]$. The first four central moments are given by:
\begin{align}
    \text{Mean:}     \quad & \mu = \E[X] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i
        && \\[4pt]
    \text{Variance:} \quad & \mu_2 = \E[(X-\mu)^2] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i-\mu)^2 \equiv \sigma^2
        && \\[4pt]
    \text{Skewness:} \quad & \frac{\mu_3}{\mu_2^{3/2}}
        = \frac{\E[(X-\mu)^3]}{\E[(X-\mu)^2]^{3/2}}
        && (\text{Standardized})\\[4pt]
    \text{Kurtosis:} \quad & \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^{2}}
        = \frac{\E[(X-\mu)^4]}{\E[(X-\mu)^2]^{2}}.
        && (\text{Standardized})
\end{align}
Skewness and kurtosis are standardized by dividing by the variance raised to the power of $\tfrac{3}{2}$ and 2, respectively, making them independent of the scale of the data.\\
Defining the residuals \mbox{$r \equiv X - \mu$}, the standardized kurtosis of a Gaussian is
\begin{align}
    \E[r^4] &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} r^4 e^{-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}} dr = 3\sigma^4 \\[4pt]
    \E[r^2] &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} r^2 e^{-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}} dr = \sigma^2 \\[4pt]
    \text{Kurtosis} &= \frac{\E[r^4]}{\E[r^2]^2} = \frac{3\sigma^4}{\sigma^4} = 3.
    \label{eq:kurtosis_gaussian}
\end{align}
From equation \ref{eq:kurtosis_gaussian} we can see that a Gaussian distribution is expected to have a kurtosis of 3, regardless of the scale of the underlying data. Excess kurtosis is defined as the kurtosis minus 3, representing the amount of kurtosis above or below the expected value for a Gaussian distribution: 
\begin{equation}
    \text{EK} = \dfrac{\mu_4}{\mu^2_2} - 3.
    \label{eq:excess_kurtosis_biased}
\end{equation}
A positive excess kurtosis indicates more samples in the tails, while a negative excess kurtosis indicates fewer samples in the tails as compared to a Gaussian distribution. Excess kurtosis can therefore be used in RFI detection to identify a spectrum that deviates from Gaussian.\\

Equation \ref{eq:excess_kurtosis_biased} is the population excess kurtosis, for a finite sample size $M$ the unbiased sample excess kurtosis is given by \citet{kendallAdvancedTheoryStatistics1969a} as
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{EK}_s = \frac{M-1}{(M-2)(M-3)} \left( (M+1) \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2} - 3(M-1) \right).
    \label{eq:excess_kurtosis_unbiased}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spectral Kurtosis for Power Spectra}

Spectral kurtosis was developed by \citet{nitaStatisticsSpectralKurtosis2010} for RFI detection in single-look power spectra. The theory of spectral kurtosis is that a power spectral density (PSD) per unit frequency, hereafter referred to as power ($P_k$) for frequency bin $k$, is proportional to voltage squared ($P_k \propto |V_k|^2$) and follows an exponential distribution. Spectral kurtosis acts as a test on the power spectrum to determine whether the underlying voltage distribution is Gaussian or not. Spectral kurtosis is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{SK} = \frac{\widehat{\sigma}_k^2}{\widehat{\mu}_k^2},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\sigma}_k^2$ is the sample variance and $\hat{\mu}_k$ is the sample mean of power in frequency bin $k$ over $M$ independent samples, given by 
\begin{align}
    \hat{\mu}_k &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} P_{k,i} \\[4pt]
    \hat{\sigma}_k^2 &= \frac{1}{M-1} \sum_{i=1}^{M} (P_{k,i} - \hat{\mu}_k)^2.
\end{align}
Power is the squared magnitude of voltage, so the spectral kurtosis applied to a power measurement is conceptually similar to measuring kurtosis of the voltage, but not identical to it. \citet{nitaStatisticsSpectralKurtosis2010} show that an unbiased estimate of spectral kurtosis can be computed using the simplified formula
\begin{align}
    \text{let:} \quad S_1 &= \sum_{i=1}^{M} P_{k,i}, \quad S_2 = \sum_{i=1}^{M} P_{k,i}^2 \\[4pt]
    \widehat{SK} &= \frac{M+1}{M-1} \left( \frac{M S_2}{S_1^2} - 1 \right).
\end{align}
For voltages drawn from a Gaussian distribution, the spectral kurtosis should be exactly 1.\\

Most systems do not record single-look power and instead record integrated power. To accommodate this
\citet{nitaGeneralizedSpectralKurtosis2010} developed a generalized spectral kurtosis estimator which acts on integrated power. The generalized spectral kurtosis estimator is given by
\begin{align}
    \text{let:} \quad S_1 &= \sum_{i=1}^{M} \sum_{j=1}^{N} P_{k,i,j}, \quad S_2 = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left( \sum_{j=1}^{N} P_{k,i,j} \right)^2 \\[4pt]
    \widehat{SK} &= \frac{N M d + 1}{M - 1} \left( \frac{M S_2}{S_1^2} - 1 \right),
\end{align}
where $N$ is the number of samples per integration, and $d$ is the shape parameter of the underlying distribution, which for an exponential distribution is 1. 

\section{DRAO RFI Monitor}
\label{sec:rfi_monitor}
\subsection{RFI Monitor Specifications}
A roof-mounted receiver located at the DRAO site, called the ``RFI monitor,'' records radio signals from all directions, providing a means of estimating the RFI that CHORD is expected to receive \cite{bruceWidebandRFIMonitor2026}. The monitor records complex voltages at a rate of 4 gigasamples per second (GSPS). The stream is processed by an oversampled polyphase filterbank (OSPFB) with a 16/15 oversampling ratio yielding 16 frequency bands each with 125~MHz bandwidth and 133~MHz sampling rate. Each band is sent through a critically sampled filterbank, which performs a Fourier transform, and converted from voltage to power. Power is integrated over 2500 time samples to obtain integrations lasting 0.75 seconds. The final data product contains 600,000 channels at 3.33 kHz resolution from 0 to 2 GHz. The data are stored in PSD units of dBm/Hz. This analysis uses one hour of RFI monitor data collected at 6:00 PM PDT on May 5, 2026 comprising 4800 time samples at 0.75 second resolution, a sample of which is plotted in figure \ref{rfi_monitor_full_slice}.

\subsection{RFI Monitor Data Statistics}
\label{sec:rfi_monitor_data_statistics}
To understand the statistics of kurtosis and spectral kurtosis we trace how statistical noise propagates through the RFI monitor. 
An analog voltage measures incoming signals, which are digitized to real and imaginary components $V_R$ and $V_I$, respectively, given by
\begin{equation}
    V = V_R + jV_I.
\end{equation}
In both statistical noise and astronomical signals, the real and imaginary components are independent and identically distributed Gaussian random variables with identical variance $\sigma^2$. The mean of each component is unknown and non-zero. However, the mean does not impact the distribution type or its kurtosis and is therefore ignored. Next, power is computed as the magnitude of voltage squared, given by
\begin{equation}
    P = |V|^2 = V_R^2 + V_I^2.
\end{equation}
The sum of the squares of two independent Gaussian random variables follows a chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom, which is equivalent to an exponential distribution with mean $2\sigma^2$, meaning that power at this stage is exponentially distributed.
\begin{align}
    P &\sim \sigma^2 \chi^2_2  \\[4pt]
    P &\sim \exp (2\sigma^2)
\end{align}
The stream is then integrated over $N$ samples, which forms a gamma distribution with shape parameter $N$ and scale parameter $2\sigma^2$.
\begin{align}
    P_N &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i \\[4pt]
    P_N &\sim \text{Gamma}(N, 2\sigma^2)
\end{align}
Finally the integrated power is converted to power in decibel units of dBm/Hz by scaling power with a gain calibration factor ($G_\mathrm{cc}$) and taking the log,
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{dB} = 10\log_{10}(G_\mathrm{cc} \cdot P_N).
\end{equation}
To perform analysis we need to recover the integrated power \mbox{$P_N$} from the data product \mbox{$P_\mathrm{dB}$} which is done by exponentiating
\begin{equation}
    P_N = \frac{10^{P_\mathrm{dB}/10}}{G_\mathrm{cc}}.
\end{equation}

The gain factor $G_\mathrm{cc}$ is unknown and changes between each calibration cycle, which occurs every 5 minutes as shown in the top panel of figure \ref{rfi_monitor_time_slice_violin}. An analysis of power at 1420~MHz, which is an RFI protected frequency, shows a statistically significant change in power between each calibration cycle (bottom panel figure \ref{rfi_monitor_time_slice_violin}), which can only be attributed to the gain factor as there is not expected to be any time-dependent signal change at 1420~MHz. Therefore, computing kurtosis of $P_N$ between calibration cycles will be overestimated as some of the variance would be caused by the changing gain factor and not the intrinsic variance of $P_N$. We split the time range into 11 distinct calibration segments of 382 samples each, equating to 4.7 minutes per segment. We discard the first and last segments shown in figure \ref{rfi_monitor_time_slice_violin} as they do not contain a complete set of 382 samples.

Within each calibration segment the gain factor remains constant and can be safely ignored, we compute spectral kurtosis of integrated power $P_N$ using the generalized spectral kurtosis estimator, knowing that Gaussian noise should have a spectral kurtosis of 1. Additionally, under the central limit theorem, the sum of $N$ independent, identically distributed, exponential random variables, converges to a Gaussian. In Appendix \ref{sec:gamma_vs_gaussian} we show that a gamma distribution with shape $N$ and scale $2\sigma^2$ converges to a Gaussian distribution with mean $N\cdot 2\sigma^2$ and variance $N\cdot (2\sigma^2)^2$. Since the number of integrated samples ($N = 2500$) is large, it is reasonable to apply the Gaussian approximation given by
\begin{equation}
    P_N \approx \text{Gaussian}(N \cdot 2\sigma^2, N \cdot (2\sigma^2)^2).
\end{equation}
By making the Gaussian approximation, we can compute excess kurtosis of the integrated power $P_N$ and expect it to be 0 for a Gaussian power distribution.

\section{Application of RFI Detection}
\label{sec:rfi_detection_application}
In this section we apply excess kurtosis to test if the power distribution is Gaussian, and spectral kurtosis to test if the underlying voltage distribution is Gaussian.

\subsection{Excess Kurtosis RFI Detection}
\label{sec:excess_kurtosis_rfi_detection}
To obtain the expected distribution of excess kurtosis under the null hypothesis of Gaussian power, we performed a Monte Carlo simulation. We drew 382 samples from a Gaussian distribution and computed excess kurtosis. We repeated this for 100,000 trials, giving the expected distribution of excess kurtosis shown in Figure~\ref{fig:excess_kurtosis_monte_carlo}. We take the RFI detection thresholds as the 0.1st and 99.9th percentiles of the resulting distribution, giving \(-0.584\) and \(1.072\) threshold bounds, respectively. The thresholds are asymmetric, which arises from the positive skew of excess kurtosis.\\

To validate the theoretical bounds, we selected a clean portion of the spectrum from 1400~MHz to 1430~MHz because it sits within an RFI-protected band. Figure \ref{fig:excess_kurtosis_1400MHz} shows the PSD spectrum of the clean band alongside its excess kurtosis, confirming that there is no significant RFI. We aggregated the observed excess kurtosis of the clean band across each calibration cycle and plotted it alongside the simulated results in figure \ref{fig:excess_kurtosis_monte_carlo}. The observed excess kurtosis closely matches theory which is expected for an RFI-free zone. The observations are slightly shifted right compared to simulated results which suggests more outliers in the true data than predicted by theory. A likely cause is low-level, intermittent, broad-spectrum RFI, which nevertheless impacts protected spectrum. We selected intentionally wide bounds of 0.1\% and 99.9\% to limit false-positives; these bounds classified 99.5\% of the observed data between 1400~MHz and 1430~MHz as RFI-free, whereas theory predicts that 99.8\% should be RFI-free.\\

\subsection{Spectral Kurtosis RFI Detection}
To apply the generalized spectral kurtosis estimator, we must specify the shape parameter $d$ and integration count $N$. The theoretical value $d$ of an exponential distribution is 1 \cite{nitaGeneralizedSpectralKurtosis2010}, and the total integration count is $N=2500$ \cite{bruceWidebandRFIMonitor2026}. However, in practice, not every integrated sample is fully independent; oversampling introduces duplicate samples, and windowing and filtering within the filterbank may induce correlation between adjacent samples. A standard practice employed by \citet{smithSimulatingSpectralKurtosis2022} and \citet{nitaSpectralKurtosisStatistics2017} is to empirically estimate the shape parameter $d$ from the data to account for these instrumental effects.\\

We reduce the integrated sample count $N$ from 2500 to \(2500 \times 15/16\) to account for the 16/15 oversampling ratio. Next, we compute spectral kurtosis on integrated power $P_N$ using the RFI-free band of 1400~MHz to 1430~MHz shown in figure \ref{fig:excess_kurtosis_1400MHz}. We compute the mode of spectral kurtosis across the clean spectrum to empirically estimate the calibrated shape parameter $d=0.96$, such that the calibrated mode sits at exactly 1, as shown in figure \ref{fig:spectral_kurtosis_histogram}.\\

We plotted the spectral kurtosis of the clean band from 1400~MHz to 1430~MHz as a histogram alongside the simulated results in figure \ref{fig:spectral_kurtosis_all_time}. The observed spectral kurtosis matches the simulated data nearly perfectly, with 99.7\% of observed data falling within the simulated 99.8\% bounds.\\

\subsection{Radio Recombination Line Visibility}
Because each calibration segment must be treated independently, the spectral kurtosis results produce 11 distinct RFI labels for each frequency bin, one from each segment. Typically there is strong agreement between segments. On average any two segments provide the same RFI labels for 93.8\% of frequency bins, with the lowest segment agreement being 91.7\% and the highest being 95.9\%. A full pairwise comparison is presented in Appendix \ref{sec:spectral_kurtosis_pair_plot}. Disagreement between segments is likely driven in part by random noise fluctuations and in part by intermittent RFI. \\

A given frequency bin is observable when it is free of RFI at least 50\% of the time. CHORD will remain at the same pointing for long periods (weeks to months), which allows for sufficient observation time in the periods when the RFI is not present. We therefore chose to label a frequency bin as clean if it is free of RFI in at least 50\% of the calibration segments. A sensitivity analysis of the 50\% threshold is presented in Appendix \ref{sec:alternative_rfi_thresholds} which shows the number of detectable RRL lines using different threshold assumptions. With the 50\% threshold, we find that 76.3\% of the spectrum is RFI-free, which is consistent with the overall estimate in figure \ref{fig:spectral_kurtosis_CHORD_range} of 75.6\%. The full CHORD spectrum, with RFI labels and the locations of hydrogen RRLs, is shown in figure \ref{fig:rfi_flagged_spectrum}. \\

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesis/plots/rfi/rfi_flagged_spectrum.png}
    \caption[RFI labels for full CHORD spectrum]{Sample RFI monitor power spectrum coloured by RFI label, the locations of hydrogen RRLs are shown as vertical dashed lines. Some spectrum with intermittent RFI is still considered clean if it is RFI-free in at least 50\% of calibration segments.}

For an RRL to be detectable, it should be measurable across a width of at least 100 km/s, and ideally up to 200 km/s. To characterize this full width rather than the line centre alone, we compute a Gaussian-weighted average of the binary RFI labels, centred on each line's rest frequency $f_0$ with a standard deviation of 100 km/s. We convert velocity widths to frequency using $\Delta f = f_0 \Delta v / c$, resulting in a wider Gaussian at higher frequencies (100 km/s equates to 30 bins at 300~MHz and 148 bins at 1500~MHz). This approach places 68\% of the weighted importance within $\pm$100 km/s of the centre and 95\% within $\pm$200 km/s. The resulting detectability score is
\begin{equation}
    s(f_0) = \frac{\sum_i w_i\, \text{RFI}_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \qquad w_i = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(f_i - f_0)^2}{2\sigma_f^2}\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_i$ is the frequency of bin $i$ and $\text{RFI}_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is its RFI label. The score is bounded by $[0, 1]$, where $1$ indicates spectrum that is completely clean and $0$ that it is completely RFI. A plot of detectability scores versus frequency for all hydrogen RRLs is shown in figure \ref{fig:h_line_clean_score}. Using a threshold that a line must have a detectability score of at least 99\%, we find that 79 of the 116 hydrogen RRLs in the CHORD band are expected to be detectable, and 37 are not. The number of detectable transitions per frequency range is presented in table \ref{tbl:RFI_lines}. If we instead consider a frequency bin clean when a minimum of one segment is RFI-free, the total number of detectable lines becomes 91. A sensitivity analysis of spectral kurtosis thresholds and combination methods is presented in Appendix \ref{sec:alternative_rfi_thresholds}.

\subsection{Analysis Limitations}
There are several important limitations to consider about conclusions from the RFI monitor:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity mismatch:} CHORD is orders of magnitude more sensitive than the RFI monitor; signals not detectable by the RFI monitor may nonetheless impede CHORD.
    \item \textbf{Beam geometry:} CHORD's beam is directional and points upwards, whereas the RFI monitor's beam is omnidirectional and points horizontally and downward. As a result, the RFI monitor may not detect RFI signals coming from above that will impact CHORD. In particular, the GNSS satellite communication band from \mbox{1164--1215}~MHz is not detected by the RFI monitor.
    \item \textbf{Limited sampling window:} This analysis is based on a single hour of RFI monitor data, which may not be representative of the long-term RFI environment.
    \item \textbf{Temporal evolution:} RFI is not static: as new RFI sources emerge, spectrum that is currently free of RFI might become corrupted.
    \item \textbf{Gaussian RFI:} Some RFI, such as spread-spectrum RFI, appears Gaussian and could be missed by this analysis.
    \item \textbf{Scope:} This analysis only considers the impact of RFI on detectability; it does not address how line broadening or the superposition of hydrogen $\beta$, hydrogen $\gamma$, helium, and carbon recombination lines might also impact detectability.
\end{itemize}
