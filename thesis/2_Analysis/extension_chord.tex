\section{Extension CHORD}
\label{sec:extension_chord}

\subsection{Candidate Site Selection}

Angular resolution of an interferometer is determined by the maximum baseline, which is the distance between the two furthest dishes. CHORD plans to include very long baselines by adding outrigger stations 3300 km and 1000 km away at Green Bank Observatory and Hat Creek Radio Observatory respectively. These outriggers are designed to provide precise localization of bright transient events like Fast Radio Bursts but can't be used for high sensitivity surveys like RRL surveys which require continuous integration over weeks to months. Instead, 16 to 32 additional dishes can be added to the DRAO site which will feed directly to the main correlator. The purpose of the additional dishes, dubbed Extension CHORD is to improve the angular resolution of the synthesized beam. The impact on sensitivity will be minimal as increasing from 512 dishes to 544 dishes only increases the sensitivity by 6 percent and so is ignored in the sensitivity analysis of section \ref{sec:sensitivity}.\\

Locations to model extension CHORD were selected by identifying areas of the site that could be developed with minimal effort. These sites include places that already have disturbed ground and pre-existing power connections. \subsection{UV coverage and Synthesised Beam}
The Fourier transform of an image decomposes the image into a sum of spatial sinusoids of different frequencies. Spatial frequencies pointing East-West in the image spaced are labeled u while spatial frequencies pointing North-South are labeled v. Each baseline of an interferometer is sensitive to a specific spatial frequency based on the distance $b_{EW}$ and $b_{NS}$ between the two dishes and the wavelength of observation $\lambda$ as given by the equations
\begin{equation}
    u = \frac{b_x}{\lambda}, v = \frac{b_y}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Shorter baselines are sensitive to large-scale spatial structures on the sky, while longer baselines provide fine details. The collection of all $u$ and $v$ values sampled is called the UV coverage. The synthesised beam, sometimes called the `dirty beam', is the shape on the sky that a telescope is sensitive to given its UV coverage. To perfectly reconstruct an image of the sky, every spatial frequency would need to be measured. An infinitly sampled UV coverage would produce a delta function synthesided beam, meaning a sinlge point on the sky could be perfectly measured. In practice there are a finite number of baselines creating a finite UV coverage, the reconstructed image will contain distortions which are visible through the synthesised beam pattern.\\



\subsection{Weighting Methods}
Sensitivity is maximized by weighting each baseline equally, which is called natural weighting. However spatial resolution can be improved by weighting longer baselines more heavily, which is called uniform weighting. A middle ground between the two is called robust weighting, which applies a tunable weight to balance sensitivity and resolution.\\
