\chapter{Radio Line Stacking}
\label{sec:line_stacking}
The principle of line stacking is that each RRL is a measurement of the same underlying physical quantities, namely the emission measure and electron temperature of the ionized gas. We can therefore treat each RRL measurement as an independent observation and reduce variance by taking the average\footnote{The Voigt profile of each line must be aligned in velocity space before averaging, which will have the effect of over sampling high frequency lines relative to low frequency lines.}. We model each observation \(x_i\) as an independent draw from a normal distribution, \(x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(T_L, \sigma_i^2)\), where \(T_L\) is the line strength and \(\sigma_i\) is the brightness temperature sensitivity\footnote{Brightness temperature sensitivity is equivalent to the RMS noise in the measurement, and the two terms are used interchangeably.} of line \(i\). In the ideal case, for \(n\) independent samples with mean \(\mu\) and common variance \(\sigma^2\), the variance of the mean is \(\sigma^2/n\). For RRLs, however, lines at different frequencies have different RMS noise, so the optimal estimator is not a simple average but a weighted average which we discuss in section \ref{sec:weighted_average}. In section \ref{sec:snr_by_region} we provide optimal stacking strategies by region. \\

\section{Inverse-Variance-Weighted Average}
\label{sec:weighted_average}

The inverse-variance-weighted average minimizes the variance of the mean when sample variances are not constant. The weighted mean \(\hat{\mu}\) and variance of the mean \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})\) are given by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mu} = \frac{\sum_i x_i w_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{1}{\sum_i w_i}
    \label{eq:weighted_avg}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.
\end{equation}
When all sample variances are equal (\(\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2\ \forall i\)), equation \eqref{eq:weighted_avg} reduces to the simple arithmetic mean.

The inverse-variance-weighted average assumes that each sample has the same expected mean. However, peak line strength is inversely proportional to frequency, \(T_L \propto \nu^{-1}\), meaning the expected mean is not constant. To account for this we rescale each line and its RMS noise to a common reference frequency \(\nu_0\). After rescaling, each observation has the same expected mean but different expected sample variances, allowing us to apply inverse-variance weighting. The rescaled line strength \(x'\), RMS noise \(\sigma'\), and weights \(w'\) are given by
\begin{equation}
    x_i' = x_i \frac{\nu_i}{\nu_0}, \quad \sigma_i' = \sigma_i \frac{\nu_i}{\nu_0}, \quad w_i' = \frac{\nu_0^2}{\sigma_i^2 \nu_i^2}.
    \label{eq:rescaled_signal_and_noise}
\end{equation}
The choice of \(\nu_0\) is arbitrary and simply changes the reference point of the estimated mean. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the stacked estimate is independent of the choice of \(\nu_0\) and is given by
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})}} = \frac{\sum_i x_i w_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Signal-to-Noise Ratio by Sky Region}
\label{sec:snr_by_region}
Peak line intensity \(T_L\) is inversely proportional to frequency, meaning that lower-frequency lines are expected to be strongest and easiest to detect. However, CHORD is also less sensitive at low frequency, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:total_noise_maps}, due to the dominant impact of background noise. It is therefore important to understand how the SNR varies across the CHORD band depending on the sky location. To compute the SNR, we take the ratio of the expected signal, which is the line intensity \(T_L\) from equation \eqref{eq:line_intensity}, to the noise, which is the total sensitivity \(\sigma_T\) from section \ref{sec:background_noise}. In this section we use the native angular resolution per line, which is the smallest possible synthesized beam for the given frequency, and a common frequency resolution of \(5 \kHz\), representing the maximum possible precision per line. We analyze smoothing in section \ref{sec:smoothed_snr}.

To visualize SNR by region, we assume a uniform line intensity of \(T_L = 10 \mK\) at 1500 MHz across the entire sky. In reality, line intensity will vary significantly across the sky, so the SNR scale is not accurate. However, the shape and maxima of the SNR-per-frequency plots are still valid and can be used to understand which lines are most important for a given sky location. The locations used are shown in Figure \ref{fig:snr_locations}, and the SNR versus frequency for each location is shown in Figure \ref{fig:snr_by_region}.\\

To understand what is driving the SNR trends by sky region, we can separate the impact of system effects (beam shape, integration time, declination) from the impact of background noise. In Figure \ref{fig:snr_system_vs_background} we plot SNR ignoring all background and SNR assuming sensitivity is equal to the background. Based on system effects alone, SNR is significantly higher at low frequency due to the compound effect of higher signal and higher sensitivity. In contrast, when sensitivity is directly proportional to background temperature, SNR is highest at high frequency where background noise is lowest. In reality, sensitivity is a combination of system effects, beam shape, and background noise, which is why SNR peaks differently depending on the sky location.

\subsection{Signal-to-Noise Ratio after Smoothing}
\label{sec:smoothed_snr}
The results of Figure \ref{fig:snr_by_region} use a common bandwidth of \( 5 \kHz \) and apply each line's native angular resolution. We showed in section \ref{sec:angular_resolution} that the synthesized beam varies from \( \sim\!21' \) at \( 300 \MHz \) to \( \sim\!4.2' \) at \( 1500 \MHz \). Therefore, each line carries a different angular resolution. Furthermore, a \( 5 \kHz \) bandwidth corresponds to a \( \sim\!5 \kms \) velocity resolution at \( 300 \MHz \) and a \( \sim\!1 \kms \) resolution at \( 1500 \MHz \), meaning there is a unique velocity sampling for each line.\\

A common approach employed by \citet{andersonGBTDiffuseIonized2021} and \citet{liuSIGGMASURVEYIONIZED2013} is to smooth each line to a common velocity and angular resolution.
We smoothed velocity resolution by applying a custom bandwidth (\(\Delta \nu\)) to each line corresponding to a common velocity resolution \( \Delta v \),
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \nu = \frac{\Delta v}{c} \nu.
\end{equation}
Velocity smoothing reduces RMS noise of high frequency lines by increasing the effective bandwidth, which impacts the point-source sensitivity of equation \ref{eq:combined_point_source_sensitivity}.\\

We smoothed angular resolution by averaging the native synthesized beam over the larger smoothed synthesized beam. For a smoothed solid angle \( \Omega_{\text{smoothed}} \), and native solid angle \( \Omega_{\text{native}} \) the number of independent samples is
\begin{equation}
    N = \frac{\Omega_{\text{smoothed}}}{\Omega_{\text{native}}}.
\end{equation}
We scale the brightness temperature sensitivity from equation \ref{eq:brightness_temperature_sensitivity} by \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) to account for the averaging of independent samples, giving
\begin{align}
\sigma_T [\text{K}] &= \frac{\sigma_{\RMS} c^2}{\Omega_{\text{native}} 2k\nu^2} \sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\text{native}}}{\Omega_{\text{smoothed}}}} \\[4pt]
&= \frac{\sigma_{\RMS} c^2}{2k\nu^2 \sqrt{\Omega_{\text{native}} \Omega_{\text{smoothed}}}}.
\label{eq:brightness_temperature_sensitivity_smoothed}
\end{align}
It is interesting to note that the smoothed brightness temperature sensitivity of equation \ref{eq:brightness_temperature_sensitivity_smoothed} is equivalent to treating the solid angle as the geometric mean of the native and smoothed solid angles. One caveat of this approach is that it assumes each synthesized beam sample is independent, when in reality noise may be correlated over spatially adjacent samples. A second caveat is that previous surveys such as \cite{andersonGBTDiffuseIonized2021} and \cite{liuSIGGMASURVEYIONIZED2013} perform angular smoothing by convolving native resolution maps with a Gaussian kernel, whereas we have performed a non-weighted boxcar average. Despite these differences, the overall relationship between smoothing and SNR remains the same.\\

We computed SNR by region using a common velocity resolution of \( 5 \kms \) and a common angular resolution of \( 22' \). The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:smoothed_snr_by_region}. SNR is significantly improved at high frequency, which is expected since both smoothing effects improve sensitivity at high frequency. Low-frequency lines still benefit from a larger primary beam and increased RRL signal strength. However, these results become outweighed by the stronger radio background at low frequency.\\

We have shown that SNR per line is not a static quantity. It varies spatially based on changing background noise, and it varies with design choices such as velocity and angular smoothing. Therefore, the optimal stacking strategy, and the decision of which lines to target, depends heavily on the specific science goals.
