\section{Radio Line Stacking}
\label{sec:line_stacking}

% (for a deeper look at line strength based on GDIGGS see \url{https://www.overleaf.com/project/68b0918d19fe364fd7b8db66})
The principle of line stacking is that each RRL is a measurement of the same underlying physical quantities, namely the emission measure and electron temperature of the ionized gas. We model each observation \(x_i\) as an independent draw from a normal distribution, \(x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(T_L, \sigma_i^2)\), where \(T_L\) is the line strength and \(\sigma_i\) is the brightness temperature sensitivity\footnote{Brightness temperature sensitivity is equivalent to the noise in the measurement and the two terms are used interchangeably} of line \(i\). In the ideal case, for \(n\) independent samples with mean \(\mu\) and common variance \(\sigma^2\), the variance on the mean is \(\sigma^2/n\). For RRLs, however, lines at different frequencies have different noise, so the optimal estimator is not a simple average but a weighted average. \\

The inverse-variance-weighted average minimizes the variance of the mean when variances are not constant. The weighted mean \(\hat{\mu}\) and variance \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})\) are given by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mu} = \frac{\sum_i x_i w_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{\sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2}{(\sum_i w_i)^2}
    \label{eq:weighted_avg}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.
\end{equation}
When all variances are equal, equation \eqref{eq:weighted_avg} reduces to the simple arithmetic mean.

Inverse-variance-weighted average assumes that each sample has the same mean, however peak line strength is inversely proportional to frequency, \(T_L \propto \nu^{-1}\), meaning the expected mean is not constant. To account for this we rescale each line and its noise to a common reference frequency \(\nu_0\). After rescaling, each observation has the same expected mean but different variances. The rescaled line strength \(x'\), noise \(\sigma'\) and weights \(w'\) are given by

\begin{equation}
    x_i' = x_i \frac{\nu_i}{\nu_0}, \quad \sigma_i' = \sigma_i \frac{\nu_i}{\nu_0}, \quad w_i' = \frac{\nu_0^2}{\sigma_i^2 \nu_i^2}.
    \label{eq:rescaled_signal_and_noise}
\end{equation}
The choice of \(\nu_0\) is arbitrary and simply changes the reference point of the estimated mean. SNR of the stacked estimate is independent of the choice of \(\nu_0\) and is given by
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})}} = \frac{\sum_i x_i w_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Signal to Noise Ratio by Region}
Peak line intensity \(T_L\) is inversely proportional to frequency, meaning that lower frequency lines are expected to be strongest and easiest to detect. However, CHORD is also less sensitive at low frequency, as seen in figure \ref{fig:total_noise_maps}, due to the dominant impact of background noise. It is therefore important to understand how the signal to noise ratio (SNR) varies across the CHORD band depending on the sky location. To compute the SNR, we take the ratio of the expected signal, which is the line intensity \(T_L\) from equation \eqref{eq:line_intensity}, to the noise, which is the total sensitivity \(\sigma_T\) from section \ref{sec:background_noise}.\\

To visualise SNR by region, we assume a uniform line intensity of \(T_L = 10 \mK\) at 1500 MHz across the entire sky. In reality, line intensity will vary significantly across the sky, so the SNR scale is not accurate. However, the shape and maxima of the SNR-per-frequency plots are still valid and can be used to understand which lines are most important for a given sky location. The locations used are shown in figure \ref{fig:snr_locations}, and the SNR vs frequency for each location is shown in figure \ref{fig:snr_by_region}.\\

To understand what is driving the SNR trends by region, we can separate the impact of system effects (beam shape, integration time, declination) from the impact of background noise. In figure \ref{fig:snr_system_vs_background} we plot SNR ignoring all background and SNR assuming sensitivity is equal to the background. Based on system effects alone, SNR is significantly higher at low frequency due to the compound effect of higher signal and higher sensitivity. In contrast, when sensitivity is directly proportional to background temperature, SNR is highest at high frequency where background noise is lowest. In reality, sensitivity is a combination of system effects, beam shape, and background noise, which is why SNR peaks differently depending on the sky location.
